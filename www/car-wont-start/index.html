Car won’t start? Find the cause step by step

Answer a few questions — what the starter does, what the dashboard shows, whether the fuel pump primes — and get the checks you can do yourself, in the order an auto electrician does them.

2. The three common cases

It only clicks

A click means the starter solenoid has engaged and the start command is getting through. Either there isn’t enough current or the starter itself is jammed. Switch on the headlights and try again: if they dim a lot, it’s the battery or terminals. Then check the thick starter cable and the engine ground strap.

It cranks but won’t start

With the ignition on, the instrument cluster should light up, the Check Engine light should come on and the Security light shouldn’t flash. Then listen for the fuel pump: it should buzz for 2–3 seconds. Everything fine — read the error codes.

Nothing at all

Complete silence means the start command doesn’t reach the starter. Often this is the in-gear start protection or a blown fuse. A completely dark dashboard means no power: check the battery terminals and the main fuse.

3. Every answer in one place

There’s a click, but the starter doesn’t crank

A click means the starter solenoid has engaged and the start command is getting through. Either there isn’t enough current or the starter itself is jammed.

  1. Switch on the headlights and turn the key again. If the headlights dim a lot, the problem is the battery or terminals.
  2. Check that the thick cable on the starter and the ground strap from the engine to the body (frame) are tight.
  3. Try the method from the tip below.

Oleg’s tip: Tap the starter housing a few times with a hammer or a tire iron — not hard, but firmly. Then try to start. If that doesn’t help, have a helper hold the key on “Start” while you tap. If it starts, the starter is worn — repair it before it fails completely.

The battery is most likely flat

The starter doesn’t get enough current: it cranks sluggishly and the lights dim. This happens especially often in frost and with batteries older than 4–5 years.

  1. Check the terminals: are they tight, is there any deposit?
  2. Jump-start from another car. Red cable: your battery positive → donor positive. Black: donor negative → bare metal on your car’s engine.
  3. Let the donor run for 5–10 minutes, then start. Crank for no longer than 10 seconds and wait 30 seconds between attempts.
  4. If the battery goes flat overnight again, there is a current drain or the alternator isn’t charging.
The starter is silent: check the lockout and the fuse

Complete silence means the start command doesn’t reach the starter. Often this is the in-gear start protection or a blown fuse.

  1. Automatic: shift to N and try again — with a burnt range sensor the starter often won’t crank in P but will in N.
  2. Manual: press the clutch all the way down. Many cars won’t start without it.
  3. Start button: press it with the brake pedal pressed.
  4. Check the starter fuse and relay (below). You can temporarily swap the relay with a neighbouring one with the same marking.
  5. If a key or lock symbol is flashing, the immobilizer may be blocking the start.
  6. Everything is fine, but the starter is silent — try the trick in the tip below.

Oleg’s tip: Checked everything and the starter is still silent? Tap its housing with a hammer and try again. Sometimes the solenoid contacts get burnt, and a light tap helps you get to a workshop.

No power: check the battery and terminals

If the dashboard is completely dark, current from the battery isn’t reaching the car. Most often the terminals or a completely flat battery are to blame.

  1. Open the hood and wiggle the battery terminals by hand. They shouldn’t be loose.
  2. If there is white or green deposit on the terminals, remove them (negative first, then positive), clean them and put them back (positive first, then negative).
  3. Switch on the headlights. If they don’t light or are very dim, the battery is flat: jump-start from another car or charge it.
  4. Check the main power fuse (below).
The immobilizer is blocking the start

A key or lock symbol means the car doesn’t recognise the key and won’t let it start. The engine may not start or may die after 1–2 seconds.

  1. Try the spare key: if it starts with it — the problem is the first key; if it doesn't start — the antenna at the ignition switch or the car's control unit.
  2. Move other keys, tags and metal key fobs away from the key — they interfere with recognition.
  3. Replace the battery in the key fob.
  4. Car with a Start button: hold the key fob right against the button and press it. On many cars there is an antenna there.
  5. Dim dashboard — check the battery: with low voltage the immobilizer may not recognize the key.
  6. If you have an alarm with remote start, disarm the car again with the alarm fob.
The car doesn’t detect the key (Start button)

It says “Key not detected” or doesn’t respond to the Start button. Most often the key battery is dead or radio interference gets in the way. Almost every such car has a spot to hold the key: its chip is read there even without a battery.

  1. Press the brake (manual: the clutch), shift to P. Hold the key right against the Start button or the key spot and press the button. Where that spot is for your make — see below.
  2. Move your phone, other keys and metal key rings away from the key, and unplug chargers and the dash cam — according to Hyundai, Kia and Nissan manuals they interfere with the key.
  3. Interference also happens near TV towers, radio stations and airports — holding the key to the key spot helps there too.
  4. Replace the key battery. The type is printed on the old one: most often CR2032, some Hyundai — CR2450, Mazda — CR2025.
  5. If the door won’t open, there’s a mechanical key hidden in the fob, and on many cars the lock is under the driver’s handle cover.
  6. If the spare key doesn’t help either, check the car battery: with a flat battery the key isn’t recognised either.
Looks like the fuel pump isn’t working

When the ignition is switched on, the pump hums for 2–3 seconds and builds pressure. If it’s silent, it isn’t getting power or it has seized.

  1. Make sure there is fuel in the tank. If the tank is almost empty, add 5–10 liters.
  2. Check the fuel pump fuse (below).
  3. Find the pump relay and swap it with a neighbouring one with the same marking. If the pump starts humming, replace the relay.
  4. Try the method from the tip below.

Oleg’s tip: Have a helper switch on the ignition while you knock with your fist or a rubber mallet on the bottom of the tank. On many cars the pump sits under the rear seat: lift the seat, open the hatch and tap the pump cover. If the pump starts humming and the car starts, it is worn — replace it soon.

The engine control unit gets no power

The starter cranks, but with the ignition on the cluster doesn’t light up or the Check Engine light is off. This means the engine control unit gets no power, and without it the injectors, ignition and fuel pump don’t work.

  1. Check the ECU and ignition fuses (below).
  2. Check the main relay (EFI MAIN or MAIN): swap it with an identical neighbouring relay and try to start.
  3. Check that the control unit connector is firmly seated and that the engine ground wire isn’t corroded.
  4. On older cars the Check Engine bulb itself may be burnt out — a scanner that connects to the unit will show that.
  5. The unit won’t communicate and there are no 5 volts at the sensors — disconnect the 5-volt sensors one at a time (pressure, throttle and pedal position, camshaft, fuel pressure): a shorted sensor drags down the 5 V line and the unit won’t even switch on.
  6. Couldn’t find the cause — see an auto electrician: the unit’s power supply needs checking.
Fuel is being supplied — a diagnosis is needed

The starter cranks, the pump works, the immobilizer isn’t interfering. The cause lies in the ignition, sensors or ECU, and only a scanner can show it.

  1. Check the ECU and ignition fuses (below).
  2. See if Check Engine is on after a start attempt. If you have a scanner, read the codes and look them up in the error code catalog.
  3. If you filled up recently, you may have got poor-quality gas.
  4. Don’t crank the starter for longer than 10 seconds at a time, pause for 30 seconds.
Read the error codes with a scanner

Everything you can check without tools works: the starter cranks, the control unit has power, the immobilizer isn’t blocking, fuel is supplied. Now the error codes in the control unit will show the cause.

  1. Find the OBD diagnostic connector — usually under the steering wheel on the left, sometimes behind a cover.
  2. Connect the scanner and turn the ignition on without starting.
  3. Read the codes and look them up in the free error code catalog: causes and what to check first.
  4. No codes — next the spark, fuel pressure and sensors need checking: that’s a job for an auto electrician.

Oleg’s tip: From Oleg’s practice: cheap ELM327 adapters sometimes “kill” the car — after connecting one it stops starting and needs repair. If you’re buying a scanner, get a Launch: it’s proven equipment.

Automatic: no crank in P — start it in N

On an automatic, the starter cranks only in P and N — the transmission range sensor (neutral safety switch, inhibitor) allows it. Its contacts burn and wear over time: in P the starter circuit stops closing, while in N it still closes. That’s why the car often starts in neutral.

Warning: Start in N only with the parking brake set and the brake pedal pressed: in neutral the car can roll.

  1. Set the parking brake, press the brake pedal, move the shifter to N and start the engine.
  2. Silent in N too — put the lever back in P, wiggle it slightly within P and try again: a burnt contact sometimes closes with a small shift.
  3. Look at the instrument cluster: does the letter on the display match the lever position? If another letter or none shows in P, the sensor is out of adjustment or the shifter cable has stretched.
  4. If it starts in N, drive as usual. When you leave the car, put the lever in P and set the parking brake.
  5. Won’t start in P or N — check the starter fuse and relay (below) and the range sensor connector on the transmission for corrosion and moisture.
  6. A scan tool may show a range sensor or P/N switch code — for example, P0705 or P0850.

Oleg’s tip: A common story: on an automatic the starter is silent in P but cranks in N, and the car starts. The range sensor contacts have burnt. You can get to a shop like this, but don’t put off the repair.

Starts and dies right away

The engine starts and stalls after a couple of seconds. The cause can be the immobilizer, the fuel supply, the throttle body or a bad contact.

  1. If it stalls after 1–3 seconds and the key symbol flashes, try the second key: that’s how the immobilizer blocks it.
  2. Check the fuses and listen for whether the fuel pump runs.
  3. Inspect the plugs and connectors, and check the battery and terminals.
  4. If it started after a battery terminal was disconnected, the cause may be a dirty throttle plate: it needs cleaning and adaptation.
  5. If it started after a throttle body cleaning or other intake work, check that all the hoses and pipes are in place.
Won’t start after rain or a car wash

After rain or a car wash, water gets onto the coils, spark plugs and connectors and into the fuse box — the engine won’t start or misfires.

  1. Dry out the ignition coils and spark plug wells, and check the leads for arcing.
  2. Look inside the underhood fuse box for water; check the fuses and relays and dry the box out.
  3. Check the main connectors — ECU, sensors, relays — and clean any corroded contacts.
  4. Clean and tighten the battery terminals, check the charge, and see whether there’s water in the air filter.
  5. From now on, don’t wash the engine with a pressure washer or a strong jet: water in the ECU corrodes the board, sometimes not right away.
Won’t start in the cold

In the cold the engine won’t start or the starter barely turns. The main thing is not to run the battery completely flat.

  1. Don’t crank the starter for long or many times in a row: the battery will go completely flat, and warming up alone won’t be enough.
  2. If you’ve jump-started it and the starter cranks normally but the engine won’t catch, it’s not just the battery: there’s no point in jump-starting over and over.
  3. If it regularly struggles to start already at −10…−15 °C (14–5 °F), check the battery, the spark or glow plugs, the fuel supply and the oil viscosity.
  4. Warming the car up on the spot often helps — a tow truck usually isn’t needed.
After a crash or a hard hit: the fuel or power safety cutoff has tripped

In a crash, or after hitting a deep pothole, a bump or a curb, the car shuts off the fuel pump for safety — so a damaged fuel line doesn’t start a fire. On some cars a crash also fires a pyrotechnic fuse at the battery and cuts all power. It’s a safety feature, not a fault: if there’s no fuel leak, it can be reset.

Warning: Inspect the car first: if you smell gasoline or it’s wet or dripping underneath, don’t turn the pump on and don’t start it — call a tow truck. If the airbags deployed, take it to a mechanic.

  1. The starter cranks but the engine won’t start and you don’t hear the fuel pump prime — find the inertia fuel pump shutoff switch and press its button until it clicks. It’s usually a red or black button on top of a small unit.
  2. Where to look: most often in the front passenger footwell behind the side trim panel or behind the glove box; sometimes under the steering wheel by the pedals behind a panel, under the hood on the firewall, or in the trunk behind the side trim. The exact spot is in the owner’s manual, in the section on the emergency fuel shutoff.
  3. Examples: Ford Focus — on the right under the dash or behind the right side panel in the passenger footwell; Ford Fiesta — in the passenger footwell behind the glove box, on some hatchbacks in the trunk behind the right trim; Ford Transit 2000–2014 and Transit Connect before 2013 — on the left by the pedals behind the lower panel; Transit from 2014, Transit Custom, Transit Connect from 2013 and Transit Courier — behind the glove box; Jaguar — on the left ahead of the door pillar under the dash, behind a trim panel with a finger hole. Some Lada, UAZ and Daewoo cars have one too — check the manual.
  4. After pressing it, turn the ignition off for 10–15 seconds and back on: the pump should hum for 2–3 seconds. Then start the engine.
  5. No button — on many modern cars the control unit itself cuts the pump on a crash signal from the airbag module. Turn the ignition off, open and close the driver’s door, wait a minute and turn it back on. If that doesn’t help, it needs a scan tool: the airbag module keeps a crash record that a mechanic clears, and sometimes the module is replaced.
  6. The car is completely dead, or the starter won’t crank with a charged battery — check the pyrotechnic fuse at the battery positive terminal or in the power fuse box next to it. BMWs with the battery in the trunk have one, for example: after an impact the terminal fires apart, the lights and radio may still work, but the engine won’t start. A fired pyro fuse can’t be reset — it’s replaced, then the airbag codes are cleared with a scan tool.

Oleg’s tip: The switch can trip without a crash — from a hard hit into a pothole or a curb, or while loading onto a tow truck. Before hunting for a complex fault, press it: it takes a minute.

The alternator isn’t charging the battery

If the battery goes flat while you drive, or the battery light is on with the engine running, the alternator isn’t charging. The car is running on what’s left in the battery and will stall before long.

Warning: If the alternator belt has snapped, don’t keep driving: on many cars the same belt drives the water pump, and the engine will overheat quickly.

  1. Switch off everything you don’t need — window and seat heaters, the heater fan, music — and drive to a shop without shutting the engine off: if you stop it, it may not start again.
  2. Look at the alternator belt: is it intact and tight, and is it squealing?
  3. If you have a voltmeter, measure at the battery terminals with the engine running. Normal is 13.5–14.8 V. Below 13.5 V the alternator isn’t charging. Above 15 V it’s overcharging: the voltage regulator is faulty, or the AGM battery was replaced without a scanner reset.
  4. Many newer cars have “smart” charging (a controlled alternator, start-stop): the voltage varies from 12.5 to 15 V — that’s normal as long as there are no faults and the battery light is off.
  5. Inspect the battery terminals and the thick cable from the alternator: a corroded or loose connection also causes undercharging.
  6. If the battery is more than 4–5 years old, load-test it: an old one may not hold a charge even with a healthy alternator.
How to tell the battery is worn out

A battery usually lasts 3–5 years. An old one may show normal voltage with no load but sag when it cranks the starter.

  1. Look for the manufacturing date on the case or label: older than 4–5 years is already a reason to test it.
  2. Measure the resting voltage 1–2 hours after a drive, with everything off: 12.6–12.8 V is charged, about 12.4 V is roughly 75 %, 12.2 V is half, 12.0 V and below is flat.
  3. Cranking test: have a helper crank the starter while you watch the voltmeter. A healthy battery stays at 9.5–10 V or above; lower means it’s weak or flat.
  4. Charge the battery with a charger and test again: if the voltage drops quickly or the starter cranks slowly again, it’s time to replace the battery.
  5. A swollen case, cracks or electrolyte leaks — replace it at once.
  6. A load tester or battery analyser check can be done at a battery shop or by an auto electrician.
  7. Choose the new battery to match the original’s capacity, cold-cranking current and polarity. On cars with start-stop, use only the same type, AGM or EFB.
  8. On many newer cars (BMW, Audi, VW, Mercedes and others) a new battery is registered with a scan tool — otherwise the car will charge it incorrectly.
  9. Replacing an AGM battery (often on cars with start-stop)? Do a “battery replacement” reset with a scanner. Otherwise the charging will be higher than it should be: as the battery ages, the control unit raises the alternator voltage so the battery lasts longer — and the new battery will be overcharged.
The battery drains by itself

Usually it’s one of three causes: a current drain, the alternator isn’t charging, or the battery has reached the end of its life.

  1. If there’s aftermarket equipment — an alarm, head unit, dashcam, tracker — start with it: disconnect it overnight and see whether the battery still goes flat. It’s the most common cause.
  2. Check whether a light stays on in the trunk, glovebox or cabin when the car is locked.
  3. If you have a voltmeter, measure at the terminals. Engine off and battery charged: about 12.6 V. Engine running: 13.5–14.8 V.
  4. Many newer cars have “smart” charging (a controlled alternator, start-stop): the voltage varies from 12.5 to 15 V — that’s normal as long as there are no faults and the battery light is off.
  5. Below 13.5 V with the engine running — suspect the alternator.
  6. If the battery is more than 4–5 years old, load-test it.
  7. Measure the parasitic draw. Turn off the ignition and everything else, and close the doors. Remove the negative terminal. Set the multimeter to DC amps with the red lead in the 10 A jack. Connect the meter between the battery’s negative post and the removed terminal.
  8. Wait for the modules to go to sleep — anywhere from a few minutes to an hour; don’t open the doors during that time. Ideally the current is 0.06 A (60 mA) or less. Noticeably more, say 0.1–0.2 A, means some module isn’t going to sleep.
  9. Without disconnecting the meter, pull the fuses one at a time. The one that makes the current drop sharply is on the circuit with the draw: check everything it powers. While the meter is connected, don’t turn on the ignition or start the engine — it will burn out the multimeter.
Flat overnight: start with the aftermarket equipment

When the battery goes flat overnight or within a day, usually some module isn’t going to sleep and keeps drawing current. Most often it’s equipment that wasn’t fitted at the factory: an alarm, an aftermarket head unit, a dashcam, a tracker, an amplifier. Start there, and check the factory modules after.

Warning: While the multimeter is connected in series, don’t turn on the ignition or start the engine — the meter will burn out. Only disconnect an alarm that blocks starting if you know how to reconnect it: without it the car may not start.

  1. Unplug everything powered from the cigarette lighter and USB overnight: dashcam, chargers, tracker, compressor. If the battery is fine in the morning, the culprit is one of them.
  2. Aftermarket head unit: turn off the ignition and lock the car. The head unit and its button lighting should go dark. If they don’t, it’s been wired to constant positive and never switches off.
  3. Remote-start alarm: check whether it goes off by itself, or relays click under the dash, while the car sits locked.
  4. Check the lights that should switch off on their own: glovebox, trunk, dome lights, vanity mirror lights in the sun visors. You can’t see the glovebox light with the lid shut: put your phone inside recording video, close it for a minute, then watch the recording.
  5. Even a single 5 W bulb draws about 0.4 A — roughly 10 Ah over a day. On a cold or undercharged battery that’s enough for the starter to barely turn over in the morning.
  6. Measure the parasitic draw. Turn off the ignition and all equipment, and close the doors. Remove the negative terminal and connect the multimeter — DC amps, red lead in the 10 A jack — between the battery’s negative post and the removed terminal.
  7. Wait for the modules to go to sleep — anywhere from a few minutes to an hour — without opening the doors. Ideally the current is 0.06 A (60 mA) or less. If it’s higher, disconnect the aftermarket equipment one item at a time (its power connector or fuse) and watch the meter: when the current drops to normal, you’ve found what isn’t sleeping.
  8. With the aftermarket equipment disconnected and the current still high, the problem is in the factory modules or the wiring. Pull the fuses one at a time: whichever one makes the current drop, look in that circuit. Sometimes a faulty alternator is what’s drawing the current.

Oleg’s tip: Aftermarket first, factory second. Most often what keeps a car from sleeping is something added later: an alarm, an aftermarket head unit, a dashcam. Disconnect it overnight — by morning you’ll know whether that’s the cause.

Questions

Why does my car click but not start?

A click means the starter solenoid has engaged and the start command is getting through. Either there isn’t enough current or the starter itself is jammed. Switch on the headlights and turn the key again. If the headlights dim a lot, the problem is the battery or terminals.

My car cranks but won’t start. What do I check first?

Turn the ignition on and look at the instrument cluster: it should light up, the Check Engine light should come on and the Security (key or lock) light shouldn’t flash. Then listen near the rear seat or the tank: the fuel pump should buzz for 2–3 seconds. If all that is fine, read the error codes.

The car won’t start but the lights work — why?

Lights need little current, the starter needs a lot. Switch on the headlights and try to start: if they dim a lot, the problem is the battery or terminals. If they stay bright and you hear only a click, check the thick starter cable and the engine ground strap.

No sound at all when I turn the key — what is it?

Complete silence means the start command doesn’t reach the starter. Often this is the in-gear start protection or a blown fuse. On an automatic, shift to N and try again; on a manual, press the clutch all the way down.

The key or lock light is flashing and the car won’t start.

A key or lock symbol means the car doesn’t recognise the key and won’t let it start. The engine may not start or may die after 1–2 seconds. Try the spare key and replace the key fob battery.
