A multimeter for drivers

How to use a multimeter to check the battery and charging, find a blown fuse and do a simple parasitic draw test.

An inexpensive digital multimeter is a driver’s best helper. In a couple of minutes it shows you whether the battery is charged, whether the alternator is working and whether a fuse is intact.

Setting up the meter

  • The black test lead always goes in the COM jack.
  • The red test lead for voltage and continuity goes in the jack marked V (often VΩ).
  • Set the mode to DC voltage: V with a straight line, or DCV. If the meter doesn’t select the range by itself, set it to 20 V.

Battery and charging

  1. Red lead on the battery positive, black on the negative.
  2. The car has sat for a few hours: normal is 12.4–12.9 V.
  3. Have someone crank the engine: the voltage shouldn’t drop below about 9.5–10 V.
  4. Engine running: 13.5–14.8 V. Switch on the headlights and the heater — the voltage may sag slightly, but it usually stays above 13 V.

If the reading with the engine running is the same as at rest, the alternator isn’t charging. If it’s consistently above 15 V, that’s overcharging, which harms the battery and the electronics. On cars with “smart” charging, go by the figures in the manual.

Checking a fuse

Method one — pull it out and test for continuity. Use continuity mode (sound or diode symbol) or resistance on the 200 Ω range. Probes on both blades: a beep or about 0 Ω — it’s good; “1” or OL on the display — it’s blown.

Method two — without removing it. Switch on the ignition, press the black probe against clean metal on the body and touch the red one to each of the two test points on top of the fuse in turn. About 12 V on both sides — it’s good. 12 V on one side and 0 on the other — it’s blown. 0 on both — this fuse isn’t getting power right now: the circuit is switched on by a different relay or only in a certain mode.

A simple parasitic draw test

If the battery goes flat after a few days parked, something is drawing current while the car is off.

  1. Switch everything off, close the doors and take the key away from the car.
  2. Move the red lead to the 10 A jack (20 A on some meters) and set the mode to DC current: an A with a straight line, or DCA.
  3. Disconnect the clamp from the battery’s negative terminal and connect the probes between the disconnected clamp and the battery negative, so that all the current flows through the meter.
  4. Wait for the control units to “go to sleep”: usually 10 to 45 minutes, depending on the car. Don’t open the doors during this time.
  5. Up to 50 mA (0.05 A) is usually considered normal; see the manual for the exact figure. Over 100 mA is a parasitic draw: pull the fuses one at a time and watch which one makes the current drop. That’s how you find the circuit at fault.

While the multimeter is connected in series in current mode, don’t switch on the ignition or start the car: the high current will burn out the meter. After measuring, put the red lead straight back in the V jack. If you touch the meter to the battery with the lead in the 10 A jack, you’ll short the battery through the multimeter.

On some cars, disconnecting the battery resets settings, and you may need the radio code or a relearn procedure. If in doubt, leave the check to a professional: they have a clamp meter that works without disconnecting the battery.

Work out how long the battery lasts with that kind of drain. A single 5 W bulb that stays on in a closed glovebox draws about 0.4 A — roughly 10 Ah a day.

Typical beginner mistakes

  • Selecting AC voltage (V with a wavy line) instead of DC.
  • Leaving the red lead in the 10 A jack.
  • Measuring the battery right after a drive — the reading comes out too high.
  • Measuring resistance in a live circuit.
  • Poking probes into airbag connectors — never do this.
Key points
  • Black lead in COM, red in V; mode — DC voltage.
  • Battery: 12.4–12.9 V at rest, no lower than 9.5–10 V while cranking, 13.5–14.8 V with the engine running.
  • A fuse is good if there’s 12 V on both sides of it or the meter beeps in continuity mode.
  • Parasitic draw: meter in series with the negative, 10 A jack, normal is usually up to 50 mA.
  • After measuring current, put the lead straight back in the V jack.

Check yourself

Which multimeter jack does the black test lead go in?
  • 10 A
  • VΩ
  • COM

Answer: COM — The black lead always stays in the common COM jack. Only the red one is moved: to V for voltage or to 10 A for current.

The engine is running, but the multimeter reads 12.5 V at the battery, the same as at rest. What does that most likely mean?
  • The alternator isn’t charging
  • Everything’s normal
  • The battery is being overcharged

Answer: The alternator isn’t charging — With the alternator working, the voltage should rise to 13.5–14.8 V. If it hasn’t risen, there’s no charging.

The ignition is on. One test point on the fuse shows 12 V, the other 0. What’s wrong with the fuse?
  • It’s good
  • It isn’t getting power
  • You need to measure its resistance right in the circuit
  • It’s blown

Answer: It’s blown — Power reaches one side but doesn’t get through to the other — so the strip inside is broken.
