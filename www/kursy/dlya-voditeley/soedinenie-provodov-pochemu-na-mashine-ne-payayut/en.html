Joining wires: why you don’t solder on a car

How to splice a wire in a car properly: why solder in a harness breaks from vibration and what to use instead — a crimped splice or a long twist under adhesive heat shrink.

A wire has chafed through, rotted at a connector or needs extending — you have to make a joint. Solder seems the most reliable. But in a car everything shakes, freezes and gets damp, and solder in a harness is a common reason the wire breaks again.

Why solder fails in a car

  • Solder wicks into the strands and makes the wire stiff. Vibration no longer flexes the wire along its length but at one point — the edge of the solder. That’s where the strands break, often under intact insulation.
  • At the edge of the solder the wire loses its flexibility and breaks from bending many times sooner than an unsoldered one.
  • A soldering iron next to the harness melts nearby insulation and connectors, and a weak iron easily makes a cold joint that doesn’t hold.
  • Acid flux slowly eats the copper under the insulation: the wire turns green and burns through.

That’s why wires in a harness aren’t soldered but crimped: most manufacturers recommend crimped connections for wiring repairs. Solder is good on a control unit’s circuit board, where everything is fixed and doesn’t flex — not in a harness that shakes with the car.

The right way: a crimp splice

  1. The new wire should be the same gauge as the original or thicker. Strip the ends to the length of the splice without nicking the strands.
  2. Pick a splice for the wire size: red — 0.5–1.5 mm², blue — 1.5–2.5 mm², yellow — 4–6 mm².
  3. Crimp it with a crimper that has the right die, ideally a ratcheting one — not with pliers.
  4. Tug on the wire: it must not pull out of the splice.
  5. Slide the adhesive-lined heat shrink on beforehand and shrink it with a heat gun, not a lighter: the glue inside seals the joint against moisture.

No splice at hand — a long twist

Strip the ends 2–3 cm and twist the strands tightly around each other along the whole length. Cover it with adhesive heat shrink, a centimeter longer on each side. No electrical tape on top.

A twist under electrical tape is only temporary: the tape unwinds, moisture gets in, the copper corrodes, and the joint heats up and burns through.

A few more rules

  • Splicing several wires in one harness? Stagger the joints a few centimeters apart so the harness doesn’t get thick and stiff in one spot.
  • Tie the repaired section to the harness with zip ties so it doesn’t flap around or rub.
  • Find out why the wire broke: it rubbed on the body, rotted at a connector, snapped in a door. Otherwise it will break again.
  • CAN bus wires are twisted in pairs — restore the twist after the repair.
  • Don’t use insulation-piercing taps: they cut some of the strands, and moisture gets in through the hole in the insulation.
  • Don’t splice airbag wiring yourself — it usually has yellow sleeving or yellow connectors; only by the manufacturer’s procedure.
Wire joints on a car aren’t soldered: it’s better to fit a splice and crimp it, or make a long twist and seal it with adhesive heat shrink.

Key points
  • Wires in a car harness aren’t soldered: solder makes the wire stiff, and vibration breaks it at the edge of the solder.
  • The right way: a splice sized for the wire, crimped with a crimper, with adhesive heat shrink on top.
  • No splice — a 2–3 cm long twist under adhesive heat shrink. Electrical tape is only temporary.
  • Find the cause of the break and tie the repair to the harness.

Check yourself

Why does solder in a car harness often fail?
  • Solder conducts poorly
  • The wire turns stiff at the edge of the solder and breaks from vibration
  • Solder can’t stand frost

Answer: The wire turns stiff at the edge of the solder and breaks from vibration — Solder wicks into the strands, so vibration flexes the wire at one point — the edge of the solder. That’s where the strands break.

What’s the best way to splice a wire in a car?
  • Twist them together under electrical tape
  • A splice sized for the wire, crimped with a crimper, with adhesive heat shrink on top
  • Solder with tape on top

Answer: A splice sized for the wire, crimped with a crimper, with adhesive heat shrink on top — A crimped splice flexes with the wire and copes with vibration, and adhesive heat shrink keeps water out.

What is adhesive-lined heat shrink for?
  • For looks
  • The glue seals the joint against moisture
  • To make the joint stiffer

Answer: The glue seals the joint against moisture — Without a seal, moisture reaches the copper and the joint corrodes, heats up and burns through.
