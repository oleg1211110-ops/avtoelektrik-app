Fuses and relays

Where the fuses and relays are in a car, how to check and replace them, what the colors mean, and why you must never fit a higher rating or bypass a fuse with wire.

When just one thing stops working — the cigarette lighter, the heater, the headlights, the wipers — the first thing to check is the fuse. It’s the simplest and cheapest check there is.

Where to look

Most cars have two or more fuse boxes: one under the hood, next to the battery, and one inside the car — under the steering wheel, on the side of the dashboard, behind the glove box or in the trunk. The layout is printed on the box lid or given in the owner’s manual. The “Assistant” in the app will tell you which fuse to check for a specific fault.

Under the hood, besides the regular fuses, there are large high-current fuses, sometimes bolted right at the battery terminal. They protect whole groups of circuits.

Colors and ratings

The rating — the current in amps at which the fuse blows — is printed on its body. Blade fuses have standard colors:

  • 5 A — tan (light brown)
  • 7.5 A — brown
  • 10 A — red
  • 15 A — blue
  • 20 A — yellow
  • 25 A — white or clear
  • 30 A — green

If the color and the number don’t match, trust the number and the diagram.

How to check and replace

  1. Switch off the ignition and the device that isn’t working.
  2. Find the right fuse using the diagram.
  3. Pull it out with the plastic fuse puller — it’s often kept in the same box.
  4. Hold it up to the light: you’ll see a metal strip inside. If it’s broken or the body has darkened, the fuse has blown.
  5. Fit a new one of exactly the same rating.

You can check a fuse without removing it, using a test light or a multimeter: there are two small metal test points on top of the fuse. How to do it is covered in the multimeter lesson.

If a new fuse blows again straight away, there’s a short in the circuit. Replacing it again is pointless — the fault has to be tracked down.

Never fit a higher-rated fuse and never bypass one with wire, foil or a paper clip. The fuse protects the wire. With a bypass, a short will burn the wiring instead — and that means a risk of a car fire.

Relay

A relay is a switch controlled by current. A small current through the coil (terminals 85 and 86) pulls in a contact and closes a high-power circuit: 30 — supply, 87 — output to the load. Five-pin relays also have 87a — it’s connected to 30 while the relay is off. The numbers are usually stamped on the case next to the pins.

A simple check for drivers is to swap a suspect relay with an identical one (same markings and diagram on the case), for example the horn relay. If the fault “moves” with it, the relay is to blame. You can hear many relays working: for example, the fuel pump relay clicks when you switch on the ignition.

Check the fuses first. Before thinking about an expensive repair, spend five minutes on the fuse box.
Key points
  • Fuse boxes are under the hood and inside the car; the diagram is on the lid or in the manual.
  • The rating is printed on the body: 10 A — red, 15 A — blue, 20 A — yellow.
  • Replace only with the same rating; if it blows again, look for a short.
  • A higher rating or a wire bypass turns a short into a fire.
  • Relays are checked by swapping in an identical one.

Check yourself

What rating is a blue blade fuse?
  • 10 A
  • 15 A
  • 20 A
  • 30 A

Answer: 15 A — By the standard color code, blue is 15 A, red is 10 A, yellow is 20 A and green is 30 A. But the number on the body always takes priority.

The heater’s 15 A fuse has blown. What should you replace it with?
  • A 25 A fuse so it doesn’t blow again
  • A piece of wire until you buy a new one
  • A new 15 A fuse

Answer: A new 15 A fuse — Only the same rating goes in. A higher rating or a wire bypass won’t protect the wiring, and in a short it can catch fire.

A new fuse blew right after it was fitted. What does that mean?
  • There’s a short in the circuit; the fault needs to be tracked down
  • It was defective — just fit another one
  • You need a bigger fuse

Answer: There’s a short in the circuit; the fault needs to be tracked down — A fuse that blows again almost always means a short or a faulty load. Replacing fuses any further is pointless.
