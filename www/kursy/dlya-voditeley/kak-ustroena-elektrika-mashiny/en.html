How a car’s electrical system works

What a car’s electrical system is made of and what each part does: battery, alternator, starter, ground, fuses, relays and control units.

A car’s electrical system isn’t magic — it’s a few understandable parts working together. If you know what each one does, you can often make sense of a problem before you even call a mechanic.

Battery and alternator

The 12-volt battery is needed mainly for starting: it runs the starter and powers the electronics until the engine fires up. Once the engine is running, the whole car is powered by the alternator — it’s driven by the engine belt and recharges the battery at the same time.

That gives you a simple check: with the engine off, a charged battery reads about 12.4–12.9 V, and with the engine running the voltage rises to 13.5–14.8 V. If it rises, the alternator is charging.

Starter

The starter is a powerful electric motor that spins the engine for a few seconds. At that moment it draws hundreds of amps from the battery. That’s why a weak battery or a corroded terminal shows up first when you start the car: the starter cranks sluggishly or just clicks.

Ground — the second wire

Most loads have a single “positive” wire running to them, and the current returns through the body and the engine. That’s what “ground” means: the battery negative is connected to the body and the engine with heavy cables.

If a ground connection corrodes or comes loose, strange things start happening: dim lights, twitching gauge needles, slow cranking, warning lights coming on. Many “mysterious” faults are simply a bad ground.

Fuses and relays

A fuse protects the wire: in a short circuit it blows before the wiring can overheat and catch fire. A relay is a switch that lets a small current control a large one: a weak signal from a button or a control unit switches on a powerful fan, the headlights or the fuel pump. Fuses and relays are grouped in boxes — under the hood and inside the car.

Control units

A modern car is run by electronic control units: the engine control unit (ECU), plus units for the transmission, ABS, airbags and body. They take in signals from sensors, decide what to do and command the injectors, ignition coils and valves. The units talk to each other over a digital bus, most often CAN. That’s why a fault in one place can light up several warning lights on the dash at once.

Wiring diagrams often use terminal numbers from the DIN 72552 standard: 30 — constant positive from the battery, 15 — positive with the ignition on, 31 — ground, 50 — starter control. Drivers don’t have to know them, but they make it easier to follow what your mechanic is saying.

If something stops working, check the fuses first.
Key points
  • The battery is for starting; while driving, the alternator powers the car.
  • Normal: 12.4–12.9 V with the engine off and 13.5–14.8 V with it running.
  • Ground is the current’s return path through the body; a bad ground causes the strangest faults.
  • A fuse protects the wiring; a relay switches powerful loads on with a weak signal.
  • Control units are linked by the CAN bus, so one fault can light up several warning lights.

Check yourself

What powers the car’s electrical system when the engine is running?
  • Alternator
  • Only the battery
  • Starter

Answer: Alternator — With the engine running, the alternator powers everything and recharges the battery too. The battery is mainly needed for starting.

What is “ground” in a car?
  • A spare battery in the trunk
  • A separate negative wire to each bulb
  • The current’s return path through the body and engine, which are connected to the battery negative

Answer: The current’s return path through the body and engine, which are connected to the battery negative — The battery negative is connected to the body and engine, and current returns through them. A poor ground connection is a common cause of strange faults.

What is a fuse for?
  • To increase the current to a load
  • To protect the wiring from overheating and fire in a short circuit
  • To save battery charge

Answer: To protect the wiring from overheating and fire in a short circuit — In a short circuit, the fuse blows first and breaks the circuit before the wire has time to heat up and catch fire.
