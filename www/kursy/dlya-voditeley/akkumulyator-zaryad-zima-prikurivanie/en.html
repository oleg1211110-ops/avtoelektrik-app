Battery: charge, winter, jump-starting

How to tell a battery’s charge from its voltage, why it lets you down in winter, and how to jump-start and charge it properly.

The battery is the most common reason a car won’t start, especially in winter. The good news: it’s easy to check its condition yourself.

Is the battery charged?

Measure the voltage at rest — after the car has sat for a few hours, ideally overnight, with nothing switched on. Right after a drive the reading will be higher than it really is.

  • 12.6–12.9 V — fully charged.
  • About 12.4 V — roughly three-quarters charged; worth topping up.
  • About 12.2 V — half charged; needs charging.
  • 12.0 V or less — almost flat; frequent deep discharges shorten battery life.

The voltage dips while the starter is cranking. If it falls below about 9.5–10 V, the battery is weak or discharged. With the engine running it should be 13.5–14.8 V. Many newer cars have “smart” charging: the alternator raises and lowers the voltage, so readings outside these figures can be normal — check the owner’s manual.

Winter

In freezing weather a battery delivers noticeably less energy, and thickened oil makes the engine harder to crank. That’s why a battery that was “just about coping” in summer is the first thing to fail in winter. A discharged battery can freeze; a frozen battery must not be charged or jump-started — warm it up indoors first.

  • Before winter, check the voltage and charge the battery if needed.
  • Before starting, switch off the headlights, the heater and any seat or window heating; with a manual transmission, press the clutch.
  • Don’t crank the starter for more than 10 seconds, then pause for 30–60 seconds.
  • Short winter trips don’t give the battery time to recharge — top it up with a charger from time to time.

How to jump-start properly

  1. Make sure both cars are 12-volt. Never jump-start a car from a 24 V truck.
  2. Switch off both engines and everything electrical. The cars must not touch each other.
  3. Red cable: first to the positive (+) terminal of the dead battery, then to the positive (+) terminal of the donor car.
  4. Black cable: first to the negative (−) terminal of the donor car, then to clean, unpainted metal on the engine or body of the car you’re starting, away from the battery (or to the ground point shown in the manual).
  5. Start the donor car and let it run for a few minutes. Check the manual for whether to switch the donor off while starting — manufacturers give different advice.
  6. Start the car. Remove the cables in reverse order: black from the ground point, black from the donor, then the red ones.

A battery gives off hydrogen, which explodes from a spark. That’s why the last connection is made away from the battery. Don’t let the clamps touch each other, don’t lean over the battery, and never jump-start a frozen or swollen battery.

How to charge

An automatic charger is the easiest option: it sets the current itself and switches off when done. If you charge manually, use a current of about one-tenth of the capacity: about 6 A for a 60 Ah battery. AGM and EFB batteries (often fitted to cars with start-stop) need a charger with the matching mode. Charge in a well-ventilated place, and connect and disconnect the clamps only with the charger unplugged.

When disconnecting the battery: negative first, then positive; when reconnecting — positive, then negative. That way a wrench that accidentally touches the body won’t cause a short. On some cars, disconnecting the battery resets settings or calls for a relearn procedure — see the manual.

Key points
  • Measure the voltage at rest: 12.6–12.9 V is a full charge, about 12.2 V is half.
  • In winter a battery gets weaker, and a discharged one can freeze; a frozen battery is never charged or jump-started.
  • Jump-starting: positive to positive, the donor’s negative to ground on the car being started, away from the battery.
  • Charge manually at about 10% of capacity, in a ventilated place with no sparks.
  • Terminals: the negative comes off first and goes back on last.

Check yourself

The car sat overnight and the multimeter reads 12.2 V at the battery. What does that mean?
  • The battery is fully charged
  • The battery is about half charged and needs charging
  • The alternator is overcharging

Answer: The battery is about half charged and needs charging — A full charge is 12.6–12.9 V. About 12.2 V is roughly half charge — starting is at risk, especially in winter.

When jump-starting, where does the last, black clamp go?
  • To the negative of the dead battery
  • To the positive of the donor car
  • To the case of the donor battery
  • To unpainted metal on the engine or body of the car being started, away from the battery

Answer: To unpainted metal on the engine or body of the car being started, away from the battery — The last connection can spark, and hydrogen builds up around the battery. That’s why it’s made to ground, away from the battery.

Can you jump-start a car from a truck with a 24 V electrical system?
  • No: 24 V can burn out the car’s electronics
  • Yes, it’s even faster
  • Yes, if you keep the cables on only briefly

Answer: No: 24 V can burn out the car’s electronics — A passenger car is designed for 12 V. Applying 24 V can destroy the control units and other electronics.
