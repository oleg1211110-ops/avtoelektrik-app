History of gasoline injection: from K-Jetronic to direct

How gasoline injection went from mechanical K-Jetronic to electronic and direct — short and to the point.

Before injection, the carburettor prepared the fuel. Injection meters it more precisely — and it came a long way over half a century. Let's look briefly at how it evolved: it helps you understand what you see on the car.

Mechanical injection: K-Jetronic (1973–1994)

Mechanical and continuous: a spring-loaded air flow sensor plate measures the airflow, and a fuel distributor delivers fuel in proportion. The injectors are spring-loaded and spray continuously while pressure holds; at first there was no lambda control. This is the «Einspritz» system — fitted to Audi, VW, Mercedes, Porsche.

Electronics arrive: L-Jetronic and KE-Jetronic

L-Jetronic (1980s) is electronic: a vane air flow meter measures the air, and the injectors are now pulsed. The LH version measures air mass with a hot-wire sensor — the forerunner of today's MAF. KE-Jetronic (1985–1993) is the same mechanical K, but with an electronic corrector and lambda control.

Single-point: Mono-Jetronic (1988–1995)

One injector above the throttle for the whole engine. The ECU reads load from the throttle position, with adaptive lambda control. Simple and cheap — for the budget cars of the time.

Port and direct injection — the modern era

Next come port injection (an injector per cylinder, ~6 bar) and direct injection (GDI/FSI/TSI: an injector straight into the cylinder, up to 350 bar), plus combined (Toyota D-4S). How they work and how to check them is in the next lessons of this course and in the Computer Diagnostics course.

Key points
  • The injection path: mechanical K-Jetronic → electronic L/KE-Jetronic → single-point → port → direct.
  • K-Jetronic is continuous and mechanical, with no lambda — the «Einspritz» system.
  • The newer the system, the more precise the metering and the higher the pressure: direct injection runs a rail up to 350 bar.
