Port and direct injection: pressure, pump, carbon

The two main types of modern gasoline injection — port and direct. How they differ in pressure, pump and faults, and what it means for diagnosis.

Today fuel is delivered in two main ways: port injection — an injector per cylinder in the manifold, and direct (GDI/FSI/TSI) — an injector straight into the cylinder. Pressure, typical faults and how to find them all depend on this.

Port injection: injector in the manifold

MPI — an injector per cylinder sprays into the intake manifold ahead of the valve. Pressure is low, about 3–6 bar, held by a single pump in the tank. Plus: fuel washes the intake valve, so there's almost no carbon. Checking is simple: rail pressure with a gauge, injector balance, fuel trims.

Direct (GDI/FSI/TSI): injector in the cylinder

The injector sprays straight into the cylinder under high pressure. For that there's a mechanical high-pressure pump driven by the camshaft; the rail holds up to ~200–350 bar and a sensor measures it. More precise metering, more power and economy. But fuel doesn't pass the intake valves — carbon builds up on them; the high-pressure pump and injectors also wear. Checking is in two circuits: the lift pump (low pressure) and the rail (high), the pressure sensor, the injectors.

Low rail pressure on direct injection reads as a lean mixture and misfires under load: the engine bogs on acceleration but is almost normal at idle. Check commanded vs actual rail pressure in the scan tool.

Combined (Toyota D-4S)

D-4S uses both port and direct injection. At low load the port side works (cleans the valves, smooth idle), at high load the direct side (power). It takes the best of both and avoids valve carbon.

Carbon on the intake valves is a well-known direct-injection problem: fuel doesn't wash them. It's cleaned by walnut blasting. Port injection doesn't have this — nothing to clean.

Key points
  • Port: low pressure (~3–6 bar), pump in the tank, fuel washes the intake valve.
  • Direct: high pressure (up to 350 bar), cam-driven high-pressure pump, carbon on the valves.
  • Low rail pressure on direct injection means lean and misfires under load; check rail pressure in the scan tool.

Check yourself

How does direct injection (GDI) differ from port injection in pressure?
  • Direct is lower — about 3 bar
  • Direct is high — up to 350 bar, needs a cam-driven high-pressure pump
  • The pressure is the same
  • Port injection has no pump at all

Answer: Direct is high — up to 350 bar, needs a cam-driven high-pressure pump — Direct injection delivers fuel straight into the cylinder, so it needs high pressure — a rail up to ~350 bar created by a cam-driven mechanical high-pressure pump. Port injection runs ~3–6 bar from the tank pump.

Why does carbon build up on the intake valves with direct injection but not with port?
  • Direct injection uses worse fuel
  • In direct injection the injector is in the cylinder and fuel doesn't wash the intake valve; in port it does
  • It depends on the spark plugs
  • Carbon forms equally in both

Answer: In direct injection the injector is in the cylinder and fuel doesn't wash the intake valve; in port it does — In port injection the injector sprays into the manifold ahead of the valve and cleans it. In direct injection fuel goes straight into the cylinder, past the valve — carbon builds up there. It's cleaned by walnut blasting.

A direct-injection car bogs under load but is almost normal at idle. Where do you start?
  • Replace the injectors right away
  • Check commanded vs actual rail pressure in the scan tool
  • Replace the spark plugs
  • Pour in an additive

Answer: Check commanded vs actual rail pressure in the scan tool — Low rail pressure on direct injection causes a lean mixture and misfires specifically under load. First check rail pressure (commanded vs actual) in the scan tool — it points to the high-pressure pump or the lift pump.
