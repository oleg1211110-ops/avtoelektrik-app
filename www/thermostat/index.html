Bad thermostat symptoms: stuck open vs stuck closed

The thermostat is a small, cheap valve that holds the coolant back until the engine warms up, then lets it flow to the radiator. When it fails it does so in one of two opposite ways, and they look like completely different problems: stuck closed, the engine overheats; stuck open, it never warms up, the heater is weak and fuel economy drops. This page is how to tell which one you have — and how to rule out the two things that fake a bad thermostat: an air pocket after a coolant change, and a lying coolant temperature sensor.

Stop now if it’s overheating.A thermostat stuck closed makes the engine overheat, and a modern aluminum engine can warp a cylinder head from a single bad overheat. If the gauge is climbing into the red, ease off, pull over as soon as it’s safe and shut it off. Never open the radiator or coolant cap while hot.

2. What the thermostat does — and why it fails two ways

From a cold start the thermostat is closed, so coolant circulates only inside the engine and it warms up fast. At operating temperature (typically around 180–195°F / 82–90°C (180–194 °F)) it opens and lets coolant flow to the radiator to shed heat. It’s a wax pellet that expands with heat to push the valve open.

Stuck closed — the valve never opens, so hot coolant can’t reach the radiator and the engine overheats. The giveaway is a hot engine with a cold upper radiator hose, and overheating that’s worst at highway speed and load.

Stuck open — the valve never closes, so coolant flows all the time and the engine never reaches full temperature. The giveaways are a gauge that never climbs to normal, a lukewarm heater, worse fuel economy, and often a P0128 code. It won’t strand you, but a cold engine wears faster and pollutes more.

3. What you can check yourself

  1. Watch the gauge from a cold start. Normal within a few minutes and steady = thermostat opening fine. Never reaching normal, or very slow, with a weak heater = likely stuck open. Climbing past normal, especially on the highway = likely stuck closed — stop.
  2. Feel the upper radiator hose (engine off and cool to start, then run it). It should stay cool until the engine warms, then get hot quickly as the thermostat opens. Hot engine but a cold upper hose = stuck closed and no flow.
  3. Check the coolant level cold, at the overflow tank and the radiator. Low coolant and air pockets cause the same gauge swings and weak heat as a bad thermostat — rule them out first, and never open a hot system.
  4. If you pulled it, test the thermostat in heating water with a thermometer: it should start to open around its rated temperature and close as it cools. Open when cold, or never opening, is bad.
  5. If a P0128 set, look at the coolant temp sensor’s live reading on a scan tool before blaming the thermostat — a sensor reading low sets the same code, and a cheap aftermarket low-temp thermostat can trip it on purpose.
  6. Replace with the correct-temperature thermostat for your engine (and a new gasket/O-ring), refill with the right coolant mix, and bleed the air out per your car’s procedure — skipping the bleed is what makes a new thermostat “fail” right away.

4. The two things that fake a bad thermostat

An airlock. Right after a coolant change, hose job or top-up, trapped air blocks flow and sits on the temp sensor — the gauge swings and the heater blows cold, exactly like a stuck thermostat. Bleed the system first and the “bad thermostat” usually disappears.

A lying coolant temp sensor. The same sensor the computer uses to run the engine also sets P0128. A sensor reading low makes the computer think the engine is cold even when the thermostat is fine. Check its live reading before buying a thermostat — it’s the cheaper part and the faster fix when it’s the culprit.

5. Common misdiagnoses to avoid

  • Replacing the thermostat for overheating that only happens at idle or in traffic — that pattern is the cooling fan, not the thermostat.
  • Replacing the thermostat for a P0128 without checking the coolant temp sensor — a sensor reading low sets the same code.
  • Fitting a colder-than-stock thermostat to “run cooler” — it keeps the engine below its design temperature, hurts fuel economy and emissions, and can set P0128.
  • Blaming the thermostat right after a coolant change — bleed the airlock first.
  • Leaving the thermostat out entirely to “fix” overheating — the engine then runs too cold, the computer stays in warm-up mode, and it masks the real cause (often the fan, pump or a head gasket).
  • Topping up coolant again and again without finding where it is going — a leak will overheat you no matter how good the thermostat is.

Questions

What are the symptoms of a bad thermostat?

Two opposite sets. Stuck closed: the engine overheats, worst at highway speed, and the upper radiator hose stays cold while the engine is hot. Stuck open: the engine never fully warms up, the heater is lukewarm, fuel economy drops, and you may get a P0128 code.

How do I know if my thermostat is stuck open or stuck closed?

Watch the temperature gauge from a cold start. Never reaching normal, or taking far too long, and a weak heater point to stuck open. Climbing past normal toward the red, especially on the highway, points to stuck closed. Feeling the upper radiator hose is the quickest check: with a stuck-closed thermostat it stays cold even as the engine gets hot.

Can a stuck-open thermostat cause a check engine light?

Yes. A thermostat stuck open (or an aftermarket low-temperature one) keeps the coolant below the temperature the computer expects, which sets P0128 — “coolant temperature below thermostat regulating temperature.” Before you replace the thermostat, make sure the coolant temp sensor reads correctly, because a lying sensor sets the same code.

Is it safe to drive with a bad thermostat?

Stuck open you can drive, but the engine runs cold, wastes fuel and wears faster, and in winter you get little heat — fix it soon. Stuck closed is the dangerous one: it overheats, and an overheating engine can warp the head in minutes, so stop and let it cool rather than drive on.

How do you test a thermostat?

On the car: from cold, the upper radiator hose should stay cool until the engine reaches operating temperature, then suddenly get hot as the thermostat opens — that “no flow, then flow” is a good thermostat working. Off the car: drop it in a pot of heating water with a thermometer; it should start opening around its rated temperature (typically about 180–195°F / 82–90°C (180–194 °F)) and close again as it cools. One that is already open cold, or never opens, is bad.

My car overheated right after I changed the coolant — is it the thermostat?

Usually not. An air pocket (airlock) after a coolant change blocks flow and fools the temp sensor, so the gauge swings and the heater blows cold — exactly like a bad thermostat. Bleed the system per your car’s procedure first; the “bad thermostat” often disappears.
