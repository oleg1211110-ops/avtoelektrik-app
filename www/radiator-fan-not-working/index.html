Radiator fan not working? How to test the cooling-fan circuit

If your car overheats sitting in traffic but cools down the moment you get moving — or the A/C blows cold on the highway and warm at a stoplight — the electric cooling fan is the first suspect. The good news is that it’s an electrical circuit, and electrical circuits are checkable in order: the fuse, the relay, the fan motor, and the “brain” that switches it on (the coolant temp sensor and the computer or fan module). This page walks that order so you fix the cheap part that’s actually broken, not the expensive one you guessed.

Moving at low speed briefly can push some air through the radiator to reach a safe stop, but the fix is to stop, not to keep going.

2. What the fan does, and the one clue that points to it

The electric cooling fan pulls air through the radiator when there isn’t enough natural airflow — at idle, crawling in traffic, or whenever the A/C is on and the condenser needs cooling. At highway speed the air does the job on its own, so the fan can be completely dead and you’d never know until you stop.

That gives the single most useful clue in cooling diagnosis: when it overheats. Hot at idle and in traffic, fine once moving → the fan. Hot at highway speed, fine around town → coolant flow (thermostat, pump, level), not the fan. Get the pattern right and you’ve already cut the job in half.

3. The circuit, cheap end to expensive end

A cooling fan is a motor switched by a relay, protected by a fuse, commanded by the computer from the coolant temp sensor (and the A/C request). Any link in that chain stops the fan, so check them in cost order rather than assuming the motor:

  • Fuse — cheapest and a common culprit. A repeatedly blown fuse means a short or a seized motor.
  • Relay — swap with an identical neighbor; if the fan runs, you’re done.
  • Fan motor — feed it 12 V directly; spins = good, no spin = dead.
  • Connector & ground — a melted or corroded motor connector fakes a dead motor on this high-current circuit.
  • Coolant temp sensor — reads low, so the computer never commands the fan; the fan is fine, the signal is wrong.
  • Fan control module / high-speed circuit — on variable-speed or two-speed fans, this is why only low speed works.

4. Step by step — what you can do yourself

  1. Confirm the symptom: let it idle until hot (watch the gauge, don’t cook it) or turn the A/C on, and look at the fan. Spinning = fine. Not spinning while hot = a real fault.
  2. Check the fan fuse first — it’s the cheapest, quickest thing. A blown fuse is the whole fault surprisingly often; replace it only with the same rating, and if the new one blows, there’s a short or a seized motor pulling too much current.
  3. Swap the fan relay with an identical neighbor (same part number/markings) in the fuse box. If the fan now runs, the relay was bad — a classic no-tools fix.
  4. Test the motor directly: unplug it and feed the motor 12 V from the battery through a fused jumper. Spins strongly = the motor is good and the fault is upstream (relay, fuse, sensor, control, wiring). No spin, or slow and noisy = the motor is worn out.
  5. Check the switch-on signal: on a scan tool, compare the engine coolant temp sensor’s live reading to the real temperature. If it reads low, the computer never commands the fan — a lying sensor, not a bad fan. Also check that the A/C-on request commands the fan.
  6. Inspect the connector at the fan motor for melting, green corrosion or a loose pin — a high-current circuit cooks a poor connection, and that fakes a dead motor. Clean or repair it before condemning the motor.
  7. Read the codes: P0480–P0482 (fan control circuits) and P0691/P0692 (fan 1 control) point at the relay, driver or wiring; coolant temp sensor codes (P0117/P0118) point at the switch-on signal. No code doesn’t clear the fan — many cars set none for a plain motor or relay failure.

5. Common misdiagnoses to avoid

  • Replacing the fan motor first — the fuse and relay are cheaper and fail more often; test the motor directly before buying one.
  • Replacing the thermostat for overheating that only happens at idle — that pattern is the fan, not the thermostat.
  • Topping up coolant to “fix” idle overheating — if the level is fine, the fan is the problem, not the coolant.
  • Condemning the fan because it doesn’t run cold — it’s supposed to stay off until the engine is hot or the A/C is on; make it hot (or turn the A/C on) before you judge it.
  • Ignoring a melted motor connector and swapping the motor — the new one melts its connector too until the wiring is repaired.
  • Assuming “no code = good fan” — a plain relay, fuse or motor failure often sets no code at all.

Questions

Why does my car overheat at idle or in traffic but not on the highway?

That is the electric cooling fan. Moving down the highway, airflow through the radiator does the cooling; stopped or crawling, the fan has to. If it overheats only when you’re stopped and cools down once you’re moving, the fan isn’t coming on — check its fuse, relay, motor and the sensor that switches it.

How do I test a radiator fan myself?

Three quick checks. 1) With the engine hot (or the A/C on), the fan should be spinning — if not, there’s a fault. 2) Swap the fan relay with an identical neighbor in the fuse box; if the fan now runs, the relay was bad. 3) Unplug the fan motor and briefly feed it 12 volts straight from the battery (fused): if it spins, the motor is fine and the fault is upstream — relay, fuse, sensor or wiring; if it doesn’t, the motor is dead.

Should the radiator fan turn on when I switch on the A/C?

Yes. On almost every car the computer commands the cooling fan on whenever the A/C compressor runs, to cool the condenser. A quick test: start the engine, turn the A/C on, and look — the fan should spin. If the A/C blows warm in traffic but cold on the move, a fan that isn’t running is a prime suspect.

What makes a cooling fan stop working?

Work from cheap to expensive: a blown fan fuse, a bad fan relay, a failed fan motor (worn brushes), a corroded or melted connector at the motor, a coolant temp sensor telling the computer the engine is cold, a bad fan control module (on cars with variable-speed fans), or broken wiring and grounds. An auto electrician checks in that order instead of guessing the motor first.

My cooling fan runs all the time, even when the engine is cold — is that bad?

It won’t overheat you, but it points to a fault: a relay stuck closed, a coolant temp sensor reading hot, a wiring short, or the computer’s “limp” default (many cars run the fan full-time on purpose when they detect a sensor or fan fault, to stay safe). Read the codes — a fan stuck on usually has a stored fan or temp-sensor code.

Can a bad coolant temperature sensor stop the fan from coming on?

Yes. On many cars the computer switches the fan on based on the engine coolant temperature sensor (the one it uses to run the engine, not just the dash gauge sender). If that sensor reads low, the computer never thinks it’s hot enough to turn the fan on, and the car overheats at idle with a “good” fan. Check the sensor’s live reading against the real temperature.
