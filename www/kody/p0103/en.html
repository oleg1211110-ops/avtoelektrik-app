P0103 — Mass or Volume Air Flow "A" Circuit High

On the diagram: Fuel and air: sensors

OP0103 means the computer is getting a mass air flow (MAF) signal that’s too high — more air than the engine could possibly be taking in. Start with the connector and wiring (a short or a bad ground can do it), then check for a dirty sensor or an intake problem. Don’t buy a sensor first.

OWhat P0103 means

P0103 is “Mass or Volume Air Flow (MAF) ‘A’ Circuit High.” The MAF sensor measures the air going into the engine, and the computer uses that to set fuel. P0103 sets when the signal is higher than the computer expects or higher than the sensor should ever put out for the conditions.

A high signal can come from the sensor itself (failed or contaminated), from wiring — a short to power, a damaged wire, or a poor ground that pushes the signal up — or, on some cars, from a restricted intake or exhaust that makes the readings not match the engine. It’s the high-side sibling of P0102 (low), P0101 (range/performance) and P0100 (general circuit).

The engine usually keeps running on an estimate, but fuel mixture can be off: typically richer, with rough running and poor economy.

Symptoms

  • Check Engine light, sometimes the only sign
  • Rough idle or an idle that hunts up and down
  • Hesitation or poor acceleration
  • Hard starting, or stalling
  • Poor fuel economy, sometimes a fuel smell or black exhaust smoke
  • Engine runs in a limp or reduced-power mode on some cars

Common causes (most to least likely)

  • A dirty, contaminated or failed MAF sensor
  • Wiring or connector fault: a short to power, a chafed or damaged wire, corroded terminals or bent pins
  • A poor or missing ground at the sensor, or a power-supply problem
  • Aftermarket intake parts, a wrong or modified MAF, or an oiled filter
  • A restricted air filter or intake, which can disturb airflow readings
  • A restricted exhaust or clogged catalytic converter (less common, but it can confuse the readings)
  • Less often, a failed computer (PCM)

What you can do yourself

  1. Read all codes and write them down. Note any other airflow or fuel trim codes (P0101, P0102, P0171, P0172) before you clear anything.
  2. Ask what was recently done. Intake work, an air filter service, a new filter or air box, or any work around the sensor can leave a connector half-seated or a wire pinched.
  3. Unplug and re-seat the MAF connector. Look for corrosion, bent or pushed-back pins, moisture, oil and damaged wires along the harness. Look for places where the wire may touch hot or moving parts.
  4. Check the air filter and the intake path for blockage, a collapsed hose or something stuck in the tube.
  5. Inspect the sensor and, if it’s dirty, clean the element with MAF-specific cleaner only and let it dry. Never touch the element, and don’t use brake or carb cleaner.
  6. If you have a scan tool, watch the live MAF reading with the key on, engine off, at idle and as you raise the revs. A reading that is already high with the engine off, or that jumps wildly, points to the sensor or its wiring. Normal values vary by engine — compare with the service manual or a known-good car of the same model.
  7. If you’re comfortable using a multimeter, check the sensor’s power and ground at the connector and look for a wire shorted to power. The correct values and pin layout depend on your vehicle — get them from the service manual by VIN.
  8. If you suspect the exhaust is restricted (lack of power at speed, a rotten-egg smell), have the exhaust checked before replacing parts.
  9. Clear the codes and drive a few trips. If it comes back after the checks, the sensor itself is the most likely culprit.

Good to know

  • Modified cars — Cold-air intakes, modified filters or a different MAF housing can send the sensor outside its expected range. Reinstalling the stock parts is the quickest test.
  • Cars with a recent repair near the intake — Look for a wire pinched under the air box or a connector left loose. Reconnecting it correctly often fixes it.
  • Specific model patterns — We didn’t find a single well-documented model pattern for P0103 in the sources we checked. Check bulletins for your car by VIN.

FAQ

Can I drive with P0103?

Usually for a short time, since the engine uses an estimate. But economy and driveability will suffer, and stalling or limp mode is possible. Don’t put it off for long.

What’s the difference between P0102 and P0103?

P0102 means the signal is too low; P0103 means it’s too high. P0103 more often points to a short to power, a ground problem or a failed sensor; P0102 more often to a dirty sensor, an open circuit or an intake leak. The first checks are the same.

Will cleaning the MAF fix P0103?

Sometimes, if contamination was the problem. If the wiring is damaged or the sensor has failed, cleaning won’t help.

Is it a wiring problem or a bad sensor?

Check the connector and harness first; a short or a bad ground is cheaper to fix than a sensor. A shop can test the signal, power and ground to tell which.

Should I replace the MAF right away?

No. Rule out the connector, wiring, air filter and intake first. If everything is fine, replace it with a quality part.

When to see a pro

  • The connector and sensor look fine and P0103 keeps coming back
  • The live MAF reading is high or erratic and you can’t tell whether it’s the sensor or the wiring
  • You suspect a shorted wire or bad ground but don’t have a multimeter or the wiring diagram
  • You suspect a restricted exhaust or catalytic converter
  • The engine stalls, goes into limp mode or the Check Engine light is flashing
