P0340 — Camshaft Position Sensor "A" Circuit (Bank 1 or Single Sensor)

OP0340 means the computer isn’t getting a good signal from the camshaft position sensor. The engine may crank for a long time, stall, or not start at all. Often it’s the sensor, its plug or oil in the connector.

OWhat P0340 means on a Toyota, Honda, Ford, Chevy or Ram

P0340 is “Camshaft Position Sensor ‘A’ Circuit (Bank 1 or Single Sensor).” The camshaft position (CMP) sensor tells the computer where the camshaft is, so it knows which cylinder is on its compression stroke and can time the fuel injection and spark. P0340 means that signal is missing, erratic or out of range.

Fact: many engines will still start with a bad cam signal because the computer can fall back on the crankshaft sensor, but it may crank longer, run rough or go into limp mode. On some engines it won’t start at all.

Don’t confuse it with the crankshaft sensor (P0335) or with a timing problem (P0016). P0340 itself is an electrical fault in the cam sensor circuit; a stretched timing chain is a different story and also needs checking if the code comes back with other cam/crank codes.

Symptoms

  • Check Engine light on (steady, sometimes flashing if it misfires)
  • Long cranking before the engine starts, or no start at all
  • Engine stalls or dies while driving, may restart after it cools
  • Rough running, hesitation or poor power (limp mode)
  • Worse gas mileage
  • On some cars the tachometer or the shift behavior act oddly

Possible causes (most to least likely)

  • A failed camshaft position sensor (heat, age, internal fault)
  • A bad connector or wiring: corrosion, a loose pin, a chafed wire, a pinched harness near the valve cover
  • Oil in the sensor plug from a leaking valve cover gasket or sensor O-ring
  • A weak battery or low voltage during cranking confusing the signal
  • A damaged or dirty reluctor/tone wheel on the camshaft, or a sensor that’s loose or the wrong gap
  • Timing chain or belt jumped or stretched (look at P0016/P0017 together)
  • A faulty crankshaft sensor or engine computer (rare)

What you can do yourself

  1. First, ask what was done recently: valve cover gasket, timing belt or chain, a new battery, or work under the hood. A plug that was knocked loose or a sensor installed crooked is a frequent culprit.
  2. Check the battery. Weak voltage during cranking can cause strange sensor codes. Clean and tighten the terminals and, if the battery is old, have it tested before replacing parts.
  3. Find the cam sensor (usually on the cylinder head or valve cover) and inspect the plug and wires. Look for oil inside the connector, green corrosion, a bent pin and rubbing on the wire. Oil in a connector is a hint that a gasket leaks, and the real repair is the gasket plus a new sensor or plug.
  4. With a scan tool during cranking, look at engine rpm. If rpm shows 0 while the starter spins, the computer isn’t getting a crank signal (P0335 territory); if rpm is fine but cam data is missing, suspect the cam sensor circuit.
  5. Check for other codes: P0335 (crank sensor), P0016/P0017 (cam/crank correlation), P0010-P0014 (cam actuator). A set of cam codes together points to timing, not just the sensor.
  6. Make sure the sensor is bolted down correctly and the O-ring is not damaged. Don’t force it; if it won’t seat, something is wrong.
  7. If you have a multimeter and the repair info, check the sensor’s power, ground and signal at the plug while cranking. If unsure, a shop does it quickly with a scope.
  8. After the repair, clear the code and drive. Make sure the engine starts normally several times.

By model

  • Engines with variable valve timing (Toyota VVT-i, Honda VTEC, Ford Ti-VCT, GM and others) — the cam position sensor and the cam phaser both work from the same signal; if P0340 appears with P0010–P0014 or P0016/P0017, check oil level, oil quality and the timing chain too
  • Many Hondas, Toyotas and Fords — the cam sensor is near the valve cover, so oil leaks from the gasket often wet the connector
  • Pickups (F-150, Silverado, Ram) — we found no single factory defect for this code; check the plug, the harness near the valve covers and the battery first
  • Specific bulletins — we don’t quote bulletin numbers here. A dealer can check your VIN for software updates or bulletins about no-start or cam signal faults

Common questions

Can I drive with P0340?

If the engine starts and runs normally, you can drive carefully, but it could stall at any moment and the car may not restart. If it cranks a long time, runs rough or stalls, don’t rely on it, especially in traffic or on the highway.

Will P0340 go away by itself?

A loose plug or a one-time glitch can clear after a few clean trips. A failed sensor or a damaged wire will return, and often leave you stuck.

Is it the cam sensor or the timing chain?

P0340 on its own points to the sensor or its circuit. If P0016 (correlation) or rattling from the chain is also present, timing is a suspect. A shop can compare the cam and crank signals on a scope.

Can I replace the cam sensor myself?

On many cars it’s a simple job: one bolt and a plug. Use a sensor made for your model, replace the O-ring, and check for oil leaks around it.

Stop and don’t drive on if:

  • The engine stalls while driving and won’t restart, or it won’t start at all and you are in traffic or in a risky place
  • The Check Engine light is flashing
  • The temperature gauge is in the red or the overheating light is on
  • The red oil pressure light is on
  • Smoke from under the hood, a burning or gas smell
  • Strong jerking, knocking from the engine, or the car barely moves
