Battery terminal corrosion: how to clean it and stop it coming back

White or blue-green crust on the battery terminals blocks the current. The car clicks, cranks slowly or won’t start — even with a good battery. Cleaning takes about 15 minutes with baking soda and water.

1. Practice

Practice: clean the terminals and start the car

Pick a car. Turn the key, then clean the terminals in the right order — and turn the key again.

    What does the corrosion look like?

    2. How to clean battery terminals

    1. Turn the engine off and take the key out. Put on gloves and eye protection — the crust contains battery acid.
    2. Disconnect the negative (−, black) cable first, then the positive (+, red). Keep the cable ends away from the posts.
    3. Mix 1 tablespoon (15 ml) of baking soda in 1 cup (240 ml) of warm water. Brush it onto the posts and clamps with an old toothbrush or a terminal brush: it fizzes as it neutralizes the acid.
    4. Scrub the posts and the inside of the clamps until you see bright metal. A terminal brush (a few dollars at any parts store) cleans both in seconds.
    5. Rinse with a little clean water and dry everything with a rag. Don’t let the water run into the battery vents.
    6. Reconnect the positive (+) first, then the negative (−). Tighten each clamp so it doesn’t turn by hand.
    7. Coat the outside of the terminals with a thin layer of dielectric grease or battery terminal spray. Anti-corrosion felt washers under the clamps help too.
    8. Start the car. If it still clicks or cranks slowly, test the battery: the corrosion may have hidden a weak battery.
    Don’t touch both terminals with a metal tool at onceA wrench across the positive and the body or the negative makes a short circuit: sparks, burns and a damaged battery. That’s why the negative comes off first.

    3. Why corrosion keeps coming back

    Corrosion comes from acid vapor escaping around the posts. It returns quickly when the battery is charged too much or too little, when a clamp is loose (the poor contact heats up), or when the battery is old and its post seals leak. Check the charging voltage with the engine running: about 13.8–14.7 V on most cars. Too high or too low — test the alternator.

    4. When cleaning isn’t enough

    The cable is stiff, swollen or green under the insulation — the corrosion has crept inside, so replace the terminal end or the cable. The battery case is wet, leaking or swollen — replace the battery. The terminals are clean, but the car still clicks: measure the voltage between the post and its clamp while someone turns the key. More than 0.1 V means the connection is still bad. Then follow the car won’t start steps, or jump start it to get going.

    Questions

    Can battery corrosion cause a car not to start?

    Yes. The crust doesn’t conduct well, so the starter can’t get enough current: you hear rapid clicking, the dash dims, or nothing happens at all — even with a good battery. In auto electrician Oleg’s practice, rapid clicking when you turn the key is almost always a weak battery or corroded terminals.

    What is the white or blue-green stuff on my battery?

    It’s corrosion from the battery acid. White or grey powder (often on the negative) is lead sulfate; a blue-green crust (often on the positive) is copper sulfate from a copper clamp. Both block current.

    Which battery terminal do I disconnect first?

    Negative (−, black) first, then positive (+, red). When you reconnect: positive first, negative last. That way a wrench touching the body can’t make sparks.

    Can I clean battery corrosion with Coca-Cola?

    It works a little because of the acid in it, but it leaves sugar behind that attracts dirt and moisture. Baking soda and water neutralizes the acid — use that.

    Does battery corrosion mean I need a new battery?

    Not necessarily. Clean the terminals and test the battery. Replace it if the case is wet, leaking or swollen, or if it fails the test.

    How do I stop battery corrosion from coming back?

    Keep the clamps tight, coat them with dielectric grease or terminal spray, use anti-corrosion felt washers, and check the charging voltage: about 13.8–14.7 V with the engine running on most cars. Charging that is too high or too low makes corrosion come back.
