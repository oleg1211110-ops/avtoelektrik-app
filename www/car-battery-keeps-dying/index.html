Car battery keeps dying? Find the parasitic drain

Dead after a night or a few days parked usually means something doesn’t go to sleep when you lock the car — an aftermarket stereo, a light that stays on, a stuck switch or the alternator. A multimeter and the fuse boxes will show you which circuit it is.

2. What to check yourself

  1. Rule out the battery and the charging first: about 12.6 V with the car off and fully charged, 13.5–14.8 V with the engine running — battery test, alternator test. A battery older than 4–5 years — have it load-tested.
  2. Aftermarket gear first, factory parts later. Unplug everything in the 12 V sockets and USB overnight — dash cam, chargers, a tracker. Fine in the morning — the culprit is one of them. An aftermarket stereo should go dark when you lock the car; if its buttons stay lit, it’s on constant power.
  3. Lights that should go out by themselves: glovebox, trunk, vanity mirrors, dome. You can’t see inside a closed glovebox — put your phone in it recording video, close the lid for a minute and watch the recording.
  4. Measure the draw. Ignition and everything off, doors closed (close the latch with a screwdriver if you need the door open), key fob away from the car. Remove the negative cable, set the multimeter to DC amps with the red lead in the 10 A jack, and connect it between the battery negative post and the cable.
  5. Wait for the modules to sleep — a few minutes to an hour, without opening the doors. Up to about 0.05 A is normal; Oleg treats 0.06 A and less as fine. 0.1–0.2 A or more — something isn’t going to sleep.
  6. Pull the fuses one at a time, in both fuse boxes, and watch the meter. The fuse that makes it drop is on the circuit with the drain — check everything that circuit feeds. If the reading jumps after you pull a fuse, wait for the modules to sleep again.
  7. No fuse helps — check the alternator. With the battery disconnected, unplug the thick wire at the alternator, connect the meter again and measure. The draw drops — a failed diode in the alternator is draining the battery.
While the meter is connected in seriesdon’t turn on the ignition or the headlights and don’t start the engine — you’ll blow the meter’s fuse or the meter itself.

    Questions

    What is a normal parasitic draw?

    After the modules go to sleep, most cars draw up to about 0.05 A (50 mA). Auto electrician Oleg treats 0.06 A and less as fine. 0.1–0.2 A or more means something isn’t going to sleep.

    How long does it take for the modules to go to sleep?

    From a few minutes to about an hour, depending on the car. Keep the doors closed and the key fob away from the car while you wait — opening a door wakes everything up again.

    Why does my car battery die after sitting for a week?

    With a normal draw a healthy battery should sit two weeks or more. If it’s dead after a week, either the draw is too high or the battery is worn out — an older battery holds far less charge. Measure the draw, then test the battery.

    Can a bad alternator drain the battery when the car is off?

    Yes. A failed diode inside the alternator can draw current while the car is parked. If no fuse makes the draw drop, disconnect the battery, unplug the thick wire at the alternator and measure again.

    Will a new battery fix a battery that keeps dying?

    Not if something is draining it — the new battery will die too. Measure the draw and check the charging voltage before you buy a battery.

    How do I find a drain without a multimeter?

    Unplug everything from the 12 V sockets and USB overnight, check that the glovebox and trunk lights go out, and switch off an aftermarket stereo or alarm if you know how to put it back. To be sure, you still need a multimeter or a DC clamp meter.
