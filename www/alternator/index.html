Bad alternator symptoms? Read the charging voltage before you buy a part

The alternator keeps the whole car alive while the engine runs — it powers the ignition, the computer, the lights and every module, and tops the battery back up. When it quits, the car runs on the battery alone until that goes flat, then it stalls and won't restart. The trouble is that a dead alternator, a dead battery and a loose belt all look the same from the driver's seat. Here are the real signs, how to tell it from the battery, the belt and the voltage regulator, and the one-minute charging check that settles it.

1. Quick read

Quick read: find your answer fast

Pick what it's doing. You get the likely cause — and whether it's really the alternator, the battery, the belt or the regulator.

2. Alternator vs battery vs belt vs voltage regulator

These four get blamed for each other constantly. The battery stores energy and starts the engine; a tired one cranks slowly and shows low voltage at rest (healthy ≈ 12.6 V rested) — it does not turn on the charge light by itself. The alternator makes the electricity once running; a battery light on with the engine running, or going flat as you drive, is the alternator. The belt spins it — loose/glazed squeals and undercharges, snapped stops charging (and on many engines also drives the water pump, so stop). The voltage regulator (built into most modern alternators) sets the output — stuck low = no charge, stuck high = overcharging (over ~15 V, which boils the battery). Two readings sort it: low at rest but 13.5–14.5 V running = battery; below ~13.5 V (or above ~15 V) running = alternator. Charging faults can set P0562 (low), P0563 (high) or P0620.

3. The charging check — about 13.5–14.5 V

With the engine running, a healthy charging system reads roughly 13.5–14.5 V at the battery terminals (some cars up to ~14.8 V, and smart-charging / start-stop cars vary on purpose between about 12.5 and 15 V with no fault codes). It's a normal range, not one magic number, so don't condemn an alternator on a single low idle reading with nothing switched on. Meter on DC volts, engine running, then load it with the headlights and heater blower at about 2,000 rpm: below ~13.5 V under load it isn't charging enough; above ~15 V it's overcharging. The step-by-step tool with example readings is on the alternator-test page.

4. What you can do yourself, step by step

  1. Confirm it's charging. Engine running, measure at the battery terminals: ~13.5–14.5 V is good; stuck near 12.3–12.6 V means it isn't charging; over ~15 V means overcharging.
  2. Check the belt — intact, tight, not squealing or glazed. A loose belt mimics a dead alternator, and a snapped belt that also drives the water pump means you stop now.
  3. Check the connections and grounds. A corroded battery terminal, a dirty engine-to-body ground, or oxidation on the thick charging cable and the alternator plug all cause undercharging. (Field point: an oxidized connector at the alternator is one of the most common reasons a good alternator "won't charge" — clean it before you buy anything.)
  4. Check the two fuses. A heavy main charging fuse on the output wire; on some older cars the field (excitation) circuit runs through the charge warning lamp with its own small fuse — a blown bulb or fuse can stop it exciting at all.
  5. Load-test the battery if it's over 4–5 years old — an old one won't hold a charge even behind a perfect alternator.
  6. If it dies overnight, measure the parasitic draw. A bad diode can leak current while parked; a drain test (meter in series on the negative cable) and pulling fuses one at a time finds it.

5. Common misdiagnoses to avoid

  • Replacing the battery when the charge light is on — a light with the engine running is the charging system, not the battery.
  • Condemning the alternator on one low idle reading on a smart-charging car — load it with the lights and blower and hold ~2,000 rpm first.
  • Buying an alternator before checking the belt, the connector and the ground — the cheap things cause most "no-charge" calls.
  • Ignoring overcharging — more than ~15 V boils the battery and damages modules; it's a regulator fault, not "a strong alternator."
  • Throwing the whole alternator at it — inside, the usual culprits are the brushes, the voltage regulator and the rectifier (diode) bridge, which an auto electrician can often repair instead.

Questions

What are the signs of a bad alternator?

The battery or charge light comes on with the engine running, the lights dim or flicker (worst at idle), the battery goes flat while you drive and the car eventually stalls, and sometimes a whine or growl from the alternator. A battery that keeps dying can be the alternator too.

What voltage should an alternator put out?

About 13.5–14.5 V at the battery terminals with the engine running (up to roughly 14.8 V on some cars). "Smart"-charging and start-stop cars vary between about 12.5 and 15 V on purpose — that's normal as long as there are no fault codes and the battery light is off.

Is it the alternator or the battery?

Measure twice. Low at rest but 13.5–14.5 V running points to the battery. Below ~13.5 V (or above ~15 V) running points to the alternator. The battery can't turn on the charge light by itself.

Can I keep driving with the battery light on?

Not far. Switch off everything you don't need and drive to where you can test or fix it without shutting the engine off — it may not restart. If the belt has snapped, stop: on many cars it also drives the water pump and the engine will overheat.

Can a bad alternator drain the battery overnight?

Yes. A failed diode in the rectifier can leak current while the car is parked. A parasitic-draw test with a multimeter shows it.

What's the difference between the alternator and the voltage regulator?

The alternator generates the electricity; the voltage regulator (built into most modern alternators) controls how much. A regulator stuck low means no charge; stuck high means overcharging — over ~15 V, which cooks the battery.
