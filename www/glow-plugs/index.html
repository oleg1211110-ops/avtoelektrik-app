Diesel glow plugs: bad symptoms, how to test, and why you replace them as a set

A diesel has no spark plugs — it lights off on heat and pressure. When the engine is cold that heat isn't there yet, so glow plugs preheat the combustion chambers until the fuel will ignite. That's why a diesel starts fine all summer and then fights you on the first cold morning: the summer-fine, winter-hard pattern is the single biggest tell that the glow system is the problem. Here is what glow plugs do, how to read the wait-to-start light, how to test them, and how to replace them without snapping one off in the head.

2. What glow plugs are — and the wait-to-start light

Glow plugs live only in diesels. Turn the key on a cold diesel and a glow / "Wait to Start" light comes on for a few seconds while the plugs heat the chambers — wait for it to go out before cranking. On modern diesels the plugs often keep working a short time after the engine starts (afterglow) to cut white smoke and smooth the first cold minutes. How long the light stays on depends on temperature: nearly instant when warm, longer in hard cold (not automatically a fault — just wait). Symptoms of bad plugs: hard/long cold cranking (fine once warm), white or gray smoke and a rough shake for the first minute, the light flashing/never coming on/staying on too long, codes P0380–P0384 (circuit) or P0670–P0684 (relay/module), and one or two burned-out plugs leaving it rough until warm.

3. How to test glow plugs yourself

  1. Watch the dash light. Key on: it should come on and go out after a few seconds (longer when cold). Never comes on, or flashes — look at the circuit and read the codes first.
  2. Check the fuse and the relay/module. Find them on your truck's wiring diagram, check the connector, and swap the relay for a known-good one of the same part.
  3. Measure each plug with a multimeter (resistance). A good glow plug reads almost 0 Ω — usually under about 2 Ω; an open (burned-out) one reads OL. Measure with the harness unplugged, one probe on the plug terminal, one on its body.
  4. Check for voltage at the plugs. Key on during preheat, the glow-plug bus should see roughly 12 V for a few seconds. Voltage there but a plug doesn't heat — that plug is bad. No voltage — it's the relay, the fuse or the wiring.
  5. Read the codes with a scanner — P0380–P0384 and P0670–P0684 name the circuit, the relay or the module, which saves guessing.
  6. Replace as a set and remove carefully. Aged plugs go together, so replace the whole set. The hard part is getting the old ones out — they seize in the head and a snapped plug turns a quick job into a big one. Let the engine warm, soak the threads with penetrant, work them loose gently with heat-cool cycles rather than force; if one feels about to break, stop and let a specialist finish. Torque the new ones to spec.
Bench-testing a removed plug (field note)On 12 V through a fuse and the right polarity, a good plug glows red from the tip in a few seconds — do it in short pulses, not continuous (steady 12 V melts the tip, and modern plugs are low-voltage, PWM-driven in the truck). Never jumper 12 V to plugs still in the engine "to get it to start" — that masks the fault and cooks the wiring and relay.

4. When it isn't the glow plugs

Glow plugs get blamed for every cold no-start, but plenty aren't preheat: gelled winter fuel waxing up and plugging the filter (cranks fine, no fuel — the most common cold no-start); a weak battery in the cold (slow cranking won't build compression heat); water in the fuel frozen in the filter (drain the separator) or air in the lines after running dry or a filter change (prime it); and hard hot, fine cold — not glow plugs at all, but the injection pump and fuel side. If you're also seeing a DEF warning or a DPF regeneration problem, chase those on their own pages.

5. Common misdiagnoses to avoid

  • Replacing glow plugs on a no-crank — no crank is batteries, grounds or the starter; glow plugs only matter once it's spinning.
  • Buying plugs for a winter no-start that cranks but gets no fuel — that's gelled fuel in the filter, not preheat.
  • Chasing plugs for a hot-only no-start — a hot engine doesn't use them; look at the injection side.
  • Jumping 12 V onto installed plugs to force a start — it masks the fault and burns the plugs, relay and wiring.
  • Replacing only the one dead plug — the rest are the same age; do the set.

Questions

What are the symptoms of bad glow plugs?

Hard or long cold starts (especially in winter), white smoke and a rough shake until it warms, a glow / wait-to-start light that flashes or never comes on, and codes P0380–P0384. In warm weather it usually starts nearly normally.

How long should glow plugs stay on before I start?

It depends on temperature — nearly instant when warm, longer in hard cold. Watch the dash light: wait for it to go out, then crank.

My glow plugs test fine but the diesel still won't start cold — why?

Check the battery (cold), the fuel (gelled or water in the filter), air in the lines, and fuel pressure. If it's only hard hot, that's the injection pump and fuel side — glow plugs aren't used on a warm engine.

How do I test a glow plug?

With a multimeter on resistance: a good plug reads almost 0 Ω (usually under ~2 Ω), an open one reads OL. Check for ~12 V at the plug bus during preheat too — voltage but no heat means a bad plug; no voltage means the relay, fuse or wiring.

Can I put 12 V straight on a glow plug?

Only to bench-test a removed plug, in short pulses through a fuse — a good one glows red from the tip in seconds. Steady 12 V melts it, and jumpering installed plugs to force a start just hides the fault.

Do I have to replace all the glow plugs at once?

It's strongly recommended — they age together, so a single new plug among old ones brings the rough cold start right back. Remove them carefully; a seized plug can snap off in the head.
